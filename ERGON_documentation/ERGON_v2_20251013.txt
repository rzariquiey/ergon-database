ERGON: A granular coding of split ergativity based on GB409 (20 sub-features)
Roberto Zariquiey and Jaime Montoya
PUCP
1. Introduction
The following is a proposal of binary coding features intended to capture the range of crosslinguistic variation of systems of ergative alignment of flagging. Grambank currently codes ergative alignments by means of a single feature (GB409, “Is there any ergative alignment of flagging?”). 390 languages are coded “1” for this feature, 1379 languages are coded “0”, and 150 are coded “?” (unknown). Nevertheless, there is evidence of a significant amount of crosslinguistic variation and complexity in ergative systems that far exceeds what can be captured by a single feature. In particular, it is usually the case that within a language there is a split in which certain factors condition an ergative alignment in some contexts and a non-ergative alignment in others; this is usually called split ergativity. Factors commonly involved in this phenomenon include the the referential properties of arguments, the degree of agency or control of the A argument, and the TAM values of the clause.
2. Ergative flagging in Georgian and Kakataibo
We will illustrate the phenomenon of split ergativity in Georgian and Kakataibo as evidence of the complexity that can be found in the ergative systems of the world.
2.1. Georgian
The following exposition is based on Hewitt’s (1995) grammar of Georgian. This language exhibits different alignment patterns of flagging conditioned by the TAM values of the clause. Three patterns are grouped into Series I, Series II and Series III “conjugations”. 
Series I conjugations (constituted by the Present Indicative, Present Subjunctive, Imperfect Indicative, Future Indicative, Future Subjunctive, and Conditional (Hewitt, 1995, p. 122)) show evidence of a nominative-accusative alignment, as illustrated in the examples in (1). S and A arguments are marked with nominative case while P arguments are marked with dative case[1]. 
1. a.        kal-i                vašl-s                mo-i-t’an-s
woman-nom        apple-dat        pv-sv-bring-3sg.sbj(fut)
‘The woman will bring the apple.’ (Hewitt, 1995, p. 125)


        b.        bavšv-i                login-ze        c’ev-s
                child-nom        bed-on                lie-3sg.sbj(prs)
                ‘The child is lying on the bed.’ (p. 125)


Series II conjugations are constituted by the Aorist Indicative and Aorist Subjunctive. Examples like the ones in (2) show an ergative-absolutive alignment, according to which the A argument is marked with ergative case while S and P arguments share the same case marker, which corresponds to the nominative case in Series I conjugations.
2. a.        lenin-ma        es        k’alam-i        ga-mo-i-q’en-a
Lenin-erg        this        pen-nom        pv-pv-sv-use-3sg.sbj.aor
                ‘Lenin used this pen…’ (Hewitt, 1995, p. 577)


        b.        avadmq’op-i                da-c’v-a
                sick_person-nom        pv-lie-3sg.sbj.aor
                ‘The sick person lay down/went to bed.’ (p. 557)


However, there are intransitive verbs that do not behave like the one in (2b) and instead take ergative marking the Series II conjugations:
3. a.        k’at’a-m        mo-šard-a
cat-erg        pv-urinate-3sg.sbj.aor
‘The cat urinated.’


        b.        jaġl-ma        da-q’ep-a
                dog-erg        pv-bark-3sg.sbj.aor
                ‘The dog barked.’


According to Hewitt (1995), these verbs belong to the “Medial” class. For Hewitt, although these verbs are intransitive on the surface, they are underlyingly transitive and thus pose no problem for the position that the Series II conjugations are characterized by an ergative-absolutive alignment in the case marking of nouns (Hewitt, 1995, p. 341). However, Hewitt lists under the category of “Medial” a variety of verbs which do not seem to be underlyingly transitive nor have a logical object at all: celkob ‘you behave naughtily’, zarmacob ‘you behave lazily’, ǰiut’ob ‘you behave obstinately’, q’oq’manob ‘you hesitate’, c’eraob ‘you screw around’, brč’q’vialebs ‘it glitters’, k’rtis ‘it glimmers’, prialebs ‘(flag) flutters’, bodialeb ‘you walk aimlessly’, čerčet’ob ‘you wander/talk mindlessly’, ġventavs ‘it drips’, cocxlob ‘you are alive’, cxovrob ‘you live’, among others. Therefore, “ergative-absolutive” does not seem to be adequate label for the alignment system of the Series II conjugations when these verbs are taken into consideration.
Series III conjugations include the Perfect, Pluperfect, and IIIrd Subjunctive (Hevitt, 1995, p. 122). They exhibit an “inversion” of case markers, as can be seen in examples (4a-c), in which the logical subject is marked with the dative case and the logical object is marked with the nominative case:
4. a.        varsken-s        ianvr-is        rva-s        p’irvel-ad
Varsken-dat        January-gen        8-dat        first-adv
u-c’am-eb-i-a                                šušanik’-i
ov.3sg.iobj-torture-ts-perf-3sg.sbj        Shushanik’-nom
‘Varsken apparently first tortured Shushanik’ on 8th January.’ (Hevvit, 1995, p. 259)


        b.        šinabera-s        jaġl-is-tvis        jval-i                mi-u-c-i-a
                spinster-dat        dog-gen-for        bone-nom        pv-ov.3sg.iobj-give-perf-3sg.sbj
                ‘The spinster apparently gave/has given a/the bone to the/a dog.’ (p. 549)


        c.        mtvral-s        (nard-i)                u-tamaš-(n)-i-a
                drunk-dat        (backgammon-nom)        ov.3sg.ioj-play-(III)-perf-3sg.sbj
                ‘The drunk apparently (has) played (backgammon).’ (p. 551)


        d.        es                male        gada-c’q’vet’-il-a
this.nom        soon        pv-decide-perf-3sg.sbj
‘This was apparently soon decided.’ (p. 308)


Example (4c) has an omissible logical object (marked with nominative case). The intransitive version of (4c) would suggest a nominative-accusative alignment when compared to the transitive constructions in (4a-b). Other intransitive verbs, however, behave like (3d) and mark their argument with nominative case. One possibility is to consider Series III conjugations to have an active alignment, according to which the S argument takes the marker for A or P according to its degree of agency, yielding sentences like (4c) and (4b). An alternative analysis would consider (4c) to be underlyingly transitive, as its verb agrees with both arguments, even if one of them can be omitted; this would make the argument marking in Series III conjugations to have an ergative-absolutive alignment (with dative case for the A argument and nominative case for S and P arguments). Nevertheless, the problem mentioned above regarding the Medial class of intransitive verbs remains an issue for the classification of the alignment of the Series III conjugations; i.e., some definitively intransitive verbs pattern like the intransitive version of (4c), despite there being no logically possible object to occur with those verbs.
2.2. Kakataibo
We now turn to the patterns of case marking in Kakataibo according to Zariquiey’s (2018) grammar. In Kakataibo, different patterns arise for nouns and for different classes of pronouns. Nouns follow an ergative-absolutive pattern; this is illustrated in (5), where the A argument in is marked with the ergative enclitic and S and P arguments are unmarked:
5. a.        Emilio=nën        ka        ‘ó                ‘a-a-x-a
                Emilio=erg        nar:3        tapir:abs        kill-pfv-3-non.prox
                ‘Emilio killed the tapir.’ (Zariquiey, 2018, p. 488)


        b.        Emilio                ka        ‘ux-a-x-a
                Emilio:abs        nar:3        sleep-pfv-3-non.prox
                ‘Emilio slept.’ (p. 488)
A different pattern appears on (non-emphatic) pronouns, which show a tripartite alignment. Pronouns in S and A functions take enclitics =s and =n, respectively, while pronouns in P function are unmarked. This is illustrated in (6).
6. a.        a=n        ka        ‘ë        mëë-a-x-a
                 3sg=a        nar:3        1sg:p        hit-pfv-3-non.prox
                 ‘He hit me.’ (Zariquiey, 2018: p. 489)


         b.        a=x        ka        ‘ux-a-x-a
                 3sg=s        nar:3        sleep-pfv-3-non.prox
                 ‘He slept.’ (p. 488)
The tripartite alignment is also found in anaphoric noun phrases. Although not an obligatory pattern, non-pronominal anaphoric topics in the S function tend to take the case marker =x ‘S’, which is obligatory for pronouns, instead of taking the (unmarked) absolutive case. Examples in (7) illustrate how marking the non-pronominal S argument with =x conveys a given or anaphoric reading.
7. a.        achushi        uni=x                ka=is=a        ‘i-akë-x-a        
                 one                man=s                nar=rep=3        be-rem.pst-3-non.prox 
                 kaxori                        tëtan-i
                 pomegranate:abs        tie-s/a>s:se
‘It is said that that one man (you know about him) was holding pomegranates a long time ago.’ (Zariquiey, 2018, p. 547)


         b.        achushi        uni                ka=is=a        ‘i-akë-x-a
                 one                man:abs        nar=rep=3        be-rem.pst-3-non.prox
                 kaxori                        tëtan-i
                 pomegranate:abs        tie-s/a>s:se
‘It is said that a/one man (indefinite or numeral) was holding pomegranates a long time ago.’ (p. 547)
In addition, there is a set of emphatic pronouns that are formed by attaching the adverbial enclitic =bi ‘same, self’ to some of the personal pronouns. These are unmarked for their grammatical function and, therefore, they follow a neutral alignment. 
8. a.        abi                ka        ‘ë        mëë-a-x-a
         3sg:emp        nar:3        1sg:p        hit-pfv-3-non.prox
         ‘He himself hit me.’


b.        abi                ka        ‘ux-a-x-a
        3sg:emp        nar:3        sleep-pfv-3-non.prox
         ‘He himself slept.’


c.         abi                ka=na                ‘ë=n                mëë-a-x-a
         3sg:emp        nar=1sg        1sg=a                hit-pfv-3-non.prox
         ‘I hit exactly him.’
However, in the case of the first person singular emphatic pronoun ‘ëbi, even though it can appear unmarked just as abi in (8), the preferred option is for it to take the case marker =x ‘S’, which produces a horizontal alignment in which the A and P arguments remain unmarked.
9. a.        ‘ëbi=x                ka        ‘ux-a-x-a
         1sg:emp=s        nar:3        sleep-pfv-3-non.prox
         ‘I myself slept.’


b.        ‘ëbi                ka        a        mëë-a-x-a
         1sg:emp        nar:3        3sg:p        hit-pfv-3-non.prox
         ‘I myself hit him.’


c.        ‘ëbi                ka=na                a=n        mëë-a-x-a
         1sg:emp        nar=1sg        3=a        hit-pfv-3-non.prox
         ‘He hit exactly me.’

3. How Grambank has handled splits
The previous descriptions of the patterns of flagging in Georgian and Kakataibo are evidence of the complexities that alignment splits can have in the world’s languages. In addition to the previously mentioned feature GB409, Grambank has four other features, presented in Table 2, which could potentially capture such splits in ergativity. These features, however, are not without problems. 
Table 2. Grambank features that can potentially capture splits in ergativity.
Id
	Feature
	GB095
	Are variations in marking strategies of core participants based on TAM distinctions?
	GB096
	Are variations in marking strategies of core participants based on verb classes?
	GB098
	Are variations in marking strategies of core participants based on person distinctions?
	GB146
	Is there a morpho-syntactic distinction between predicates expressing controlled versus uncontrolled events or states?
	

First, the features are defined so as to include marking strategies by both indexing and flagging. It would be typologically relevant to instead distinguish between indexing and flagging when discussing alignment possibilities. Addtionally, GB146 includes any controlled/uncontrolled split, even outside the domain of argument marking. 
Second, these features group together different conditions which would be better captured by a different feature for each. For instance, we would like to know whether an alignment split in a certain language is based on either the tense, aspect or mood of the clause; such distinctions are impossible to capture just with GB095.
And finally, these features don’t capture which values of a particular category are associated with ergative marking and which ones with other marking strategies. For example, if a language has an ergative alignment in nouns and an accusative alignment in pronouns, we would like to code it differently from a language (if there is one) in which nouns have the accusative alignment and pronouns the ergative alignment. This would prove to be fruitful for the testing of hypotheses about how alignments are distributed in different contexts.
What is therefore needed is a granular approach to ergativity that divides the range of split ergative systems into more fine-grained features.
4. Proposal
Our proposal contains 22 features that are intended to capture different types of ergative splits. They also can be used in order to confirm hypotheses regarding the marked and unmarked values of categories that condition ergative marking within a split system.
Coding these features requires searching for relevant information and examples within different sources, including grammars and grammar sketches:
* In chapters or sections related to clause syntax or clause structure, look for how arguments are flagged in intransitive and transitive clauses.
* In chapters or sections related to noun morphology, look for case markers (if there are any in the language).
* In chapters related to word classes, look for nouns, pronouns, determiners and find out whether they take case marking. Look for adpositions (if there are any in the language) and find out whether they function as case markers.
* If the source is a dictionary, look for a chapter about grammar (if there is any) at the start or end of the dictionary.
* Sources available in Glottolog might not always be up-to-date. In cases you need more information in order to answer the features, a search in Google may reveal there are more recently published sources.


1. Is there an overt marker for the absolutive?
* Is an overt (phonologically non-empty) marker used to flag both S and P arguments, differently from the flagging for the A argument (i.e. is there a non-null absolutive marker)?
* The marking can be phonologically free or bound. 
* If there are phonologically free pronouns with suppletive absolutive and ergative forms, code 1 and mention the suppletion in the Comments column.
* In the case of split flagging systems (in which a certain factor conditions different marking strategies), consider the distribution of markers in each context. Code 1 if S and P are flagged by the same overt marker in any of those contexts.


2. Is there ergative alignment of flagging on first person pronouns?
* Do first person pronouns exhibit ergative alignment by means of case or adpositions?
* The marking can be phonologically free or bound. It can be overt or null.
* If only a subset of first person pronouns exhibits an ergative alignment (e.g. only first person singular pronouns), code 1 and add the additional restriction in the Comments column.
* If first person pronouns exhibit ergative alignment but other, independent conditions restrict this alignment to certain environments (e.g. only to past-tense clauses), code 1.


3. Is there ergative alignment of flagging on second person pronouns?
* Do second person pronouns exhibit ergative alignment by means of case or adpositions?
* The marking can be phonologically free or bound. It can be overt or null.
* If only a subset of second person pronouns exhibits an ergative alignment (e.g. only second person singular pronouns), code 1 and add the additional restriction in the Comments column.
* If second person pronouns exhibit ergative alignment but other, independently-defined conditions restrict this alignment to certain environments (e.g. only to past-tense clauses), code 1.


4. Is there ergative alignment of flagging on third person pronouns?
* Do third person pronouns exhibit ergative alignment by means of case or adpositions?
* The marking can be phonologically free or bound. It can be overt or null.
* If only a subset of third person pronouns exhibits an ergative alignment (e.g. only third person singular pronouns), code 1 and add the additional restriction in the Comments column.
* If third person pronouns exhibit ergative alignment but other, independently-defined conditions restrict this alignment to certain environments (e.g. only to past-tense clauses), code 1.


5. Is there ergative alignment of flagging on full NPs?


* Do non-pronominal NPs exhibit ergative alignment by means of case or adpositions?
* The marking can be phonologically free or bound. It can be overt or null.
* If non-pronominal NPs exhibit ergative alignment but other conditions, independent of the pronoun/NP distinction, restrict this alignment to certain environments (e.g. only to past-tense clauses, or only to animate NPs), code 1.


6. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in the imperfective aspect?
* This question aims to capture whether an ergative alignment of flagging (case and adpositions) occurs in clauses in the imperfective aspect. 
* The argument markers can be phonologically free or bound. They can be overt or null.
* If there are other, independently-defined conditions (e.g. tense, agency of A, etc.) that restrict the ergative alignment of flagging to certain environments, code 1 if imperfective clauses show ergative alignment of flagging in any of those environments.
* The presence/absence of ergative alignment could be conditioned by other aspectual categories similar to the imperfective (continuous, progressive, etc.). If the ergative alignment of flagging is present/absent according to one of these aspectual categories, code either 1 or 0 accordingly for this feature and add the relevant category in the Comments column.

* If there are other, independently-defined conditions (e.g. aspect, agency of A, etc.) that restrict the ergative alignment of flagging to certain environments, code 1 if past clauses show ergative alignment of flagging in any of those environments.

10. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in the indicative mood?
* This question aims to capture whether an ergative alignment of flagging (case and adpositions) occurs in clauses in the indicative mood.

11. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in non-indicative moods?
* This question aims to capture whether an ergative alignment of flagging (case and adpositions) occurs in clauses in non-indicative moods.
* For this feature, consider non-assertive speech acts such as the interrogative and the imperative. A related but distinct category is modality (i.e. the speaker’s attitude towards the realization of the event: possibility, neccesity, permission, or obligation). Some languages may combine mood and modality within the same inflectional paradigm. Consider modal distinctions only insofar as they belong to the same paradigm as mood.
* The argument markers can be phonologically free or bound. They can be overt or null.
* If the language has multiple non-indicative moods and only a subset of them exhibits an ergative alignment, code 1 and add this additional restriction in the Comments column.
* If there are other, independently-defined conditions (e.g. aspect, agency of A, etc.) that restrict the ergative alignment of flagging to certain environments, code 1 if non-indicative clauses show ergative alignment of flagging in any of those environments.
* The realis/irrealis distinction is not covered by feature.


12. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in the realis status?
* This question aims to capture whether an ergative alignment of flagging (case and adpositions) occurs in clauses in the realis status, i.e. realized events in the present and the past: “The realis portrays situations as actualized, as having occurred or actually occurring, knowable through direct perception.” (Mithun, 1999, p. 173).
* The argument markers can be phonologically free or bound. They can be overt or null.
* If there are other, independently-defined conditions (e.g. tense, agency of A, etc.) that restrict the ergative alignment of flagging to certain environments, code 1 if realis clauses show ergative alignment of flagging in any of those environments.


13. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in the irrealis status?
* This question aims to capture whether an ergative alignment of flagging (case and adpositions) occurs in clauses in the irrealis status, i.e. unrealized states of affairs: “The irrealis portrays situations as purely within the realm of thought, knowable only through imagination.” (Mithun, 1999, p. 173). The irrealis is used to refer to future events, negated events, hypothetical and counterfactual situations, commands, obligations and purposive complements (Michael, 2014).
* The argument markers can be phonologically free or bound. They can be overt or null.
* If there are other, independently-defined conditions (e.g. tense, agency of A, etc.) that restrict the ergative alignment of flagging to certain environments, code 1 if irrealis clauses show ergative alignment of flagging in any of those environments.


14. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in the context of an agentive A?
* This question aims to capture whether an ergative alignment of flagging (case and adpositions) occurs in clauses with an agentive A argument. Prototypical agents are volitional and instigate the situations in which they participate rather than be affected by them (Næss, 2007).
* Code 1 if S and P arguments are flagged in the same way, differently from agentive A arguments. Non-agentive (i.e. less prototypically agentive) A arguments are not covered by this feature.
* Split intransitivity is the phenomenon where the S argument is marked as either the A argument or the P argument according to factors such as its degree of agency. In such cases, consider the distribution of markers for each possibility of S-marking relative to a transitive clause with an agentive A argument. Code 1 if an ergative alignment of flagged occurs in any of such contexts.
* The argument markers can be phonologically free or bound. They can be overt or null.
* If there are other, independently-defined conditions (e.g. tense, aspect, etc.) that restrict the ergative alignment of flagging to certain environments, code 1 if transitive clauses with an agentive A show ergative alignment of flagging in any of those environments.
* The presence/absence of ergative alignment could be conditioned by specific categories related to agency of A (control vs. lack of control, affected agents vs. non-affected agents, active vs. stative events, etc.). If the ergative alignment of flagging is present/absent according to one of these categories, code either 1 or 0 accordingly for this feature and add the relevant category in the Comments column.


15. Is there ergative alignment of flagging in the context of a non-agentive A?

Code 1 if an ergative alignment of flagged occurs in any of such contexts.

If the ergative alignment of flagging is present/absent according to one of these categories, code either 1 or 0 accordingly for this feature and add the relevant category in the Comments column.
